THE CHRONICLE EDITORIAL VERDICT • RETROSPECTIVE & HORIZON

AI MAY CHANGE THE SHAPE OF IT — NOT END IT

Reflecting upon historical disruptions from electronic computation to tabular spreadsheets: how tools that multiply human speed expand whole industries rather than abolish them.

When Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston invented VisiCalc in 1979, panic surged through financial institutions across the globe. Ledger clerks, calculating bookkeepers, and paper accountants foresaw instant obsolescence. Why employ an entire basement of ledger scribes when an Apple II computer could recalculate thirty columns of compounding interest in less than three seconds?

The outcome confounded pessimistic prognosticators. By drastically lowering the marginal transaction cost of calculating cash-flow permutations, the electronic spreadsheet made economic modeling ubiquitous. Instead of destroying bookkeeping, it birthed the modern investment banking industry, risk arbitrage, private credit valuation, and an order of magnitude more financial analysts than had ever prepared ink ledgers.

The software industry now arrives at an analogous civilizational precipice. For four decades, Indian information technology operated on the logic of physical mechanical translation: transmuting business requirements delivered from North American boardrooms into painstakingly formatted imperative code. That specific operational layer—the bulk transcription of syntax—is unambiguously surrendering to autonomous intelligence.

Yet the universe of unmet computational need remains virtually infinite. When the marginal cost of producing software falls toward zero, the aggregate volume of systems humanity demands does not remain static; it explodes exponentially. Micro-enterprises, municipal hospitals, logistics depots, and municipal transport networks that could never justify a five-million-dollar systems development budget will deploy localized autonomous agent meshes.

The historical tragedy would be to mistake the medium for the trade. Software engineering was never about typing syntax into a text editor; it has always been about the formal decomposition of complexity, the defense of data integrity, and the governance of critical systems.

THE LESSON OF JEVONS' PARADOX IN CODE PRODUCTION

William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that improved efficiency in coal usage increased, rather than reduced, total coal consumption. In identical fashion, as generative foundation models reduce the cost per line of debugged system code, global enterprise consumption of algorithmic architecture is forecasted to multiply sevenfold by 2030.
